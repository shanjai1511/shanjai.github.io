The Second-Order Effect

The fix worked. That's exactly the problem.

Widen a road to ease traffic, and traffic gets worse. Not immediately — for a while, it's better, and everyone who advocated for the widening feels vindicated. Then more people start driving that route, because it's now the fast one, until the road is exactly as congested as before, just with more cars on it. The fix didn't fail. The fix worked so well that it changed the number of people trying to use it.

This is the first-order trap: judging an intervention by what happens right after it, in the part of the system you were watching. The second-order effect shows up later, somewhere you weren't watching, because the system had a chance to react to your reaction.

Systems respond to your response

The reason second-order effects are so consistently missed isn't complexity. It's timing. A first-order effect is fast and local — you changed X, and Y moved, and you were looking at Y. A second-order effect is slow and displaced — the system absorbed the change, adjusted around it, and the consequence shows up in Z, three weeks later, in a part of the org that wasn't in the meeting.

Add a metric to catch a specific failure, and people will stop failing that specific way — and start optimizing for the metric instead, which is a different thing that happens to look similar for a while. Add friction to slow down a risky action, and the people most affected are the careful ones who read the extra step, while the reckless ones route around it. The intervention worked exactly as designed. The system just wasn't finished responding when you called it a success.

A fix that only ever gets evaluated at the moment it's applied will look correct every single time.

The tell is in the timeline, not the data

You can't spot a second-order effect by staring harder at the same dashboard. You spot it by asking a different question: not "did this work," but "who or what had to change their behavior once this stopped being new?" That question points you at the part of the system that had time to adapt — competitors, users, the team downstream — rather than the part that merely reacted.

In practice this means deliberately checking back later, past the point where the win already got written up. Most organizations don't do this, not out of laziness, but because the incentive to look ends exactly when the metric turns green. The second-order effect is, structurally, everyone's problem to notice and no one's job to check.

What to do with this

Not every fix needs a six-month retrospective. But the ones worth that scrutiny share a pattern: they change what it's rational for other people to do next. If your fix alters an incentive, a constraint, or a visible signal that others act on, assume the system hasn't finished responding yet — and put a date on the calendar to come back and actually look.
